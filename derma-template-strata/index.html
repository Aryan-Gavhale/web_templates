Everything we treat happens in the first four millimetres.

Depth decides almost every question in dermatology: what a thing is, whether it matters, what will reach it, and what it will take to remove it. This clinic is arranged the way the skin is — from the surface down — because that is the order in which the answers arrive.

0.02 mm · Stratum corneum

The only layer you can inspect yourself. Here is what to look for.

Mark the features below and watch what they do to an ordinary spot. This is the same list a dermatologist runs through, in the same order, before reaching for a dermatoscope.

4 mm six months ago

Mark what applies

    Nothing marked. Round, one colour, even edge, small, unchanged — this is what the overwhelming majority of your spots look like, and none of them need an appointment.

    A drawing is not a diagnosis. One feature is reason enough to have a spot looked at, and a flat, featureless mark that is new deserves the same appointment as a dramatic one.

    0.10 mm · Epidermis and basal layer

    Three cancers, one tenth of a millimetre apart.

    They behave completely differently, and the difference is almost entirely a matter of which cell they started in and how far down it sat.

    0.10 mm · basal keratinocyte

    Basal cell carcinoma

    The commonest cancer in humans and the least dangerous of the three. It grows locally, erodes rather than spreads, and is almost always cured by removing it. A pearly bump that bleeds and scabs over and over is the classic story.

    Excision · curettage · topical · Mohs referral

    0.05 mm · squamous cell

    Squamous cell carcinoma

    Arises higher in the epidermis, often out of an actinic keratosis, and can spread if it is left. Tender, firm, growing, frequently on a scalp, ear, lip or forearm — the surfaces that have taken forty years of sun.

    Excision with margin · histology always

    0.10 mm · melanocyte

    Melanoma

    The rarest of the three and the one the whole examination is built around. While it is confined above the basement membrane it is cured by excision. What changes the conversation is depth — measured in millimetres, on page three of the pathology report.

    0.60 mm · Dermis

    Below the basement membrane, dermatology stops being about the surface.

    Vessels, collagen, sebaceous glands and follicles. The conditions that live down here are the ones people have usually been treating from the outside for years without much happening.

    Follicular · 0.4 — 1.2 mm

    Acne & isotretinoin

    The problem is in the sebaceous unit, not on the skin, which is why washing harder has never helped anybody. Graded at the first visit and treated to a written schedule with an end date. Where isotretinoin is warranted it is started and monitored monthly, not held back for years.

    Vascular · 0.3 — 0.8 mm

    Rosacea

    Dilated dermal vessels and inflammation, not sensitivity and not hygiene. Treated by subtype, because the flushing kind and the papular kind respond to entirely different things and are routinely given the same cream.

    Barrier & immune · 0.05 — 0.6 mm

    Eczema & patch testing

    Dermatitis that has not settled gets tested rather than re-creamed. The European baseline series plus occupational allergens, read at 48 and 96 hours, and you leave with a printed list of what to avoid rather than another tube.

    Deep follicular · 1.0 — 3.0 mm

    Hidradenitis suppurativa

    Years of being told these are boils. It is a deep follicular disease with a staging system and real treatments, including biologics where the criteria are met. Staged at the first visit so that progress is a number.

    Follicular · 1.0 — 4.0 mm

    Hair & scalp

    Scarring and non-scarring loss are different diseases with different windows for treatment, and telling them apart usually needs a scalp biopsy rather than a long conversation. Bloods on the day.

    Full thickness

    Paediatric skin

    Birthmarks, infantile haemangiomas, molluscum, and the very large category of childhood rashes that turn out to be nothing. Longer appointments and no waiting room.

    3.00 mm · Where a millimetre changes the plan

    In melanoma, the number that matters is a depth.

    Breslow thickness is measured from the top of the granular layer to the deepest tumour cell. It is the single strongest predictor of outcome, and it is why a lesion is excised rather than sampled.

    00.81.02.04.0 mm

    Under 0.8 mm
    Wide local excision and nothing else. Survival at this thickness is very close to that of the general population.
    0.8 — 1.0 mm
    The threshold at which a sentinel node biopsy is usually discussed. A tenth of a millimetre either side changes the conversation.
    1.0 — 2.0 mm
    Wider margins, node sampling, imaging, and follow-up moves from a yearly appointment to a schedule.
    Over 2.0 mm
    Oncology involvement and staging. Still treatable, and still a very different year than it would have been at 0.6 mm.

    Set out here because patients are handed these numbers without being told what they mean. Nothing on this page is a treatment plan: thickness is one of several factors, and the decisions belong to you and the specialist in front of you.

    In the rooms

    Excision under local anaesthetic

    Marked, measured and photographed before anything is cut, taken down to fascia, and closed. Every specimen goes to histopathology with an orientation suture. Results are given by telephone within five working days — never by letter, and never withheld until an appointment.

    Onward

    When it leaves this clinic

    Facial lesions needing staged margin control, anything requiring a sentinel node, and anything over 2 mm goes to Rigshospitalet with the images, the measurements and a telephone call the same day. You are not handed a referral and left to arrange it.
