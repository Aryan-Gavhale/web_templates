The first visit

Five things happen. That is all.

Read it together on the sofa before you come. Children who know the order of things tend to walk in already bored of being worried.

Step 1 of 5

  1. You come in and say hello.

    There is a box of books, a fish called Trevor, and nobody in a mask yet. You can keep your shoes on.

    For grown-ups: arrive five minutes early, bring the red book or NHS number if you have it, and don’t promise a treat — it tells a child there is something to survive.

  2. The chair goes up and down.

    You get a go at the buttons first. It is the best chair in Bristol and it tips back like a rocket seat.

    For grown-ups: for a first visit under four, we often do the whole check on your lap instead. Nothing is lost by it.

  3. We take a photo of your teeth.

    A little camera on a stick. It tickles a bit. Then you can see your own teeth on the telly, which is very strange.

    For grown-ups: intra-oral photos only at this stage. X-rays are never taken at a first visit and never without talking to you first.

  4. We count them all out loud.

    Every single one, with the numbers said properly. Most people your age have twenty. Some have a gap where one has escaped.

    For grown-ups: you get the full chart before you leave — decay risk, spacing, bite, and whether an orthodontic referral is worth thinking about yet.

  5. You pick a sticker.

    The jar lives on the desk. There are dinosaurs, space ones, and a rainbow one that everybody wants. Then you go home.

    For grown-ups: the visit takes about twenty minutes. We book the next one before you go, at six or twelve months depending on what we found.

Two whole minutes

Nobody brushes for long enough. Let us count for you.

Press the button and brush along. It splits the two minutes into four corners of your mouth, thirty seconds each, so you always know where to be.

  • Twice a day, and the night one matters more than the morning one.
  • Spit, don’t rinse. Water washes the fluoride straight back off.
  • A smear under three, a pea after that. Grown-up squeezes the tube.
  • You brush for them until seven. Let them go first, then you finish it.

Questions parents actually ask

Usually in the car park, quietly.

When should a first visit be?

As soon as the first tooth appears, or by their first birthday. It sounds absurdly early. That is the point — nothing happens, so nothing is frightening later.

She screams before we even park.

Then we do nothing on the first visit. Sit in the chair, press the buttons, go home. We have never met a child who could not be got there in three goes.

Do you do needles?

Only when a filling genuinely needs it, and never as a surprise. Numbing gel first, and the child is told exactly what they will feel, in the words they use.

He is autistic. Can you cope?

Yes, and gladly. Send us what works — the words to avoid, the lights, the order of things — and we will book a longer, quieter slot and keep the same clinician every time.

Are baby teeth worth fixing?

Yes. They hold the space for the adult ones, and a child in tooth pain does not sleep, eat or concentrate. Ignoring them costs more later.

Can I stay in the room?

Always, for any age. We will sometimes ask you to sit slightly behind, because a child watching your face for a reaction cannot relax.
